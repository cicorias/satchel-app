The quiet economics of ferries

Why the boats that cost the most to run are the ones a coastline can least afford to lose.

Imogen Fairweather
Oct 09, 2026
142 38 Share

Most of the island ferries I have written about this year lose money on every sailing. That sounds like a scandal until you look at what happens to an island when the boat stops coming, and then it starts to look like the cheapest public service there is.

The arithmetic is simple. A crew of five, a tank of marine diesel, harbour dues at both ends, and a hull that needs a dry dock every other year. Divide that by the handful of people on a February crossing and the cost per passenger is eye-watering.1

But the cost of not running the boat lands somewhere else. Teachers and nurses stop taking jobs on the island. The shop gets one delivery a week instead of three. Within a decade the school roll halves, and the council is paying far more to keep a shrinking community supplied than it ever spent on the ferry.

What the spreadsheets miss

The operators I spoke to all said the same thing in different words: the subsidy is not paying for the boat, it is paying for the island to remain a place where people can live. That is a political choice, and a reasonable one, but it gets hidden when the ferry shows up in the budget as a loss-making transport line.2
