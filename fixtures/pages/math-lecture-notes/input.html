Lecture 7: Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Notes revised 17 February 2026.

1. Definition

Let A be an n×n matrix. A nonzero vector v is an eigenvector of A if multiplying by the matrix only stretches it, so that Av=λv for some scalar λ, called the eigenvalue.

Rearranging, we need (A−λI)v=0 to have a nonzero solution, which happens exactly when the matrix is singular:

det(A−λI)=0

The left-hand side is a polynomial of degree n in λ, the characteristic polynomial. Its roots are the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.

2. A worked example

Take the matrix below. Its characteristic polynomial factors neatly, which is not typical, but it keeps the arithmetic short.

A=(4123)A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}A=(4 1; 2 3)

Expanding gives λ2−7λ+10=(λ−5)(λ−2)\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)λ²−7λ+10=(λ−5)(λ−2), so the eigenvalues are 5 and 2. Substituting each back into A−λI and solving gives one eigenvector for each.

3. Diagonalization

When an n×n matrix has n linearly independent eigenvectors, we can collect them as the columns of a matrix P and write

A=PDP−1

where D is diagonal with the eigenvalues on its diagonal. Powers then become easy, since Ak=PDkP−1 and raising a diagonal matrix to a power only raises each diagonal entry.
